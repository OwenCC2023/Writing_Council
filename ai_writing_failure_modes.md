# Known Failure Modes of AI Writing
*A working reference document — last updated March 2026*

---

## Preface

This document catalogs the known, recurring failure modes of AI-generated writing, with particular attention to fiction. It is descriptive, not exhaustive. The field moves quickly, and some failure modes present in current models may be mitigated in future ones — but the patterns below are well-established enough to constitute a working taxonomy.

Entries are organized into three tiers: **structural failures** (problems of form and architecture), **voice and style failures** (problems of language and texture), and **fiction-specific failures** (problems unique to narrative and imaginative work).

---

## Part I: Structural Failures

These are failures at the level of organization — how a piece is built rather than how it sounds.

### 1.1 The Compressed Arc

AI writing tends to resolve too quickly. In long-form work, dramatic tension that should build across chapters or scenes is frequently collapsed into a single exchange. In short fiction, a story that requires three movements often gets two. The model appears to anticipate narrative completion and accelerates toward it, treating pacing as inefficiency.

**Symptom:** Stories that feel "rushed" despite hitting all expected plot beats. The events are present; the weight of them is not.

### 1.2 Premature Resolution

Related to compressed arc but distinct: AI writing has a strong pull toward closure and comfort. Ambiguity, irresolution, and earned unhappiness are systematically underrepresented. Endings tend toward reconciliation, revelation, and restoration.

**Symptom:** Stories that arrive at clean endings when the premise demanded something messier. Antagonists explained and redeemed. Grief processed in a paragraph.

### 1.3 The Three-Act Skeleton Without Flesh

AI reliably produces structurally correct work — setup, conflict, resolution, all in approximately the right order. What it struggles to produce is texture between the load-bearing elements: the digression that reveals character, the scene that exists only for mood, the conversation that goes nowhere because that's how conversations go.

**Symptom:** Structurally correct but emotionally thin. Reads like a pitch document for a story rather than the story itself.

### 1.4 Symmetrical Structure Fetishism

AI writing has a strong aesthetic preference for parallelism and symmetry: scenes that mirror each other, characters paired as foils, motifs that return exactly once. This is a legitimate craft technique, but AI applies it reflexively, producing structures that feel schematic rather than discovered.

**Symptom:** Work that feels more designed than written. Every element accounts for another element. Nothing is wasted, which means nothing feels alive.

### 1.5 Listicle Brain

When given an analytical or expository task, AI defaults to list and bullet-point structures even when the material calls for prose argument. Ideas that have complex relationships to each other get flattened into parallel items. Nuance is reduced to additional line items.

**Symptom:** Reports, essays, and analyses that organize information without synthesizing it. Points that should build on each other instead sit side-by-side.

## Part II: Voice and Style Failures

These are failures at the sentence level — in diction, tone, and the texture of language.

### 2.1 The AI Dialect

There is a recognizable cluster of words and phrases that appear at elevated frequency in AI-generated text. They function as a kind of tell — individually unremarkable, but collectively identifying. The list shifts as models are updated, but some persistent offenders as of 2026:

- *Delve* (dramatically overused in analytic contexts)
- *Tapestry* (as metaphor for complexity)
- *Nuance / nuanced* (used as a substitute for actual nuance)
- *Importantly* (as a sentence opener)
- *It's worth noting that...*
- *At its core...*
- *Serves as a reminder that...*
- *Stands as a testament to...*
- Hyphenated compound modifiers that stack adjectives: *thought-provoking, heart-wrenching, deeply-felt*

**Symptom:** Prose that triggers immediate pattern recognition in anyone who reads AI writing regularly.

### 2.2 The Affective Safety Net

AI writing tends to name emotions rather than render them, and to name them in a particular register of measured, acknowledged, appropriate feeling. Characters feel things "deeply." Moments are "bittersweet." Difficult subjects are handled with "complexity and care." This language functions as a hedge — it acknowledges that feeling is occurring without committing to a specific, possibly wrong, emotional experience.

**Symptom:** Stories about grief that aren't sad. Stories about joy that aren't funny. The reader is told what to feel but doesn't feel it.

### 2.3 Tonal Homogenization

Within a piece, AI writing maintains a consistent register that modulates very little. In literary fiction, tone is a dynamic instrument — sarcasm undercuts sentiment, bathos punctures grandeur, a sudden flat sentence can be devastating after rich prose. AI tends to maintain a single middle temperature throughout, reliable but inert.

**Symptom:** Prose that is competent and consistent and never surprising. Nothing lands because nothing has any real weight against its surroundings.

### 2.4 The Corporate Warmth Register

In business, marketing, and general-purpose writing, AI defaults to a tone that is simultaneously friendly and professional, direct and inclusive, confident and non-presumptuous. This register is recognizable from HR communications, brand copy, and customer success emails. It is technically inoffensive and practically unreadable.

**Symptom:** Content that feels like it was produced by a content team operating under brand guidelines.

### 2.5 Hedge Stacking

AI writing accumulates qualifications. A single claim might be hedged with *generally*, *in many cases*, *it's often argued that*, *while perspectives vary*, and *ultimately* all in the same sentence. Each hedge individually is defensible; collectively they produce prose that communicates nothing.

**Symptom:** Paragraphs that could be summarized as "sometimes some people think some things, though others disagree."

### 2.6 Pseudo-Profundity

AI writing reaches for weightiness through abstract language, balanced formulations, and the grammatical shape of insight without the substance: *"In the end, it is not the destination but the journey that defines us."* These constructions pattern-match on aphorism and philosophical statement. They are not wrong, exactly. They are empty.

**Symptom:** Closing lines and transitional reflections that feel meaningful for about two seconds.

### 2.7 Adverb Dependency

AI writing relies heavily on adverbs to establish tone and intensity: *spoke softly, smiled warmly, gazed sadly*. This is the prose equivalent of labeling emotions (see 2.2) — it tells the reader the register of an action without performing it.

**Symptom:** Overloaded -ly adverbs attached to dialogue tags and action beats.

### 2.8 "Particular" as False Precision

AI writing reaches for the word *particular* as a precision-signaling device. It implies that what follows is specific, observed, and exactly right — but it almost never is. The word does the work of specificity without providing any. Examples from a single piece of fiction: *the particular chaos of too many Weasleys*, *the particular stillness that meant something mattered*, *a particular kind of exposure*, *the particular gravity of small, real things*, *the particular expression of controlled fury*.

None of these are more precise for the word *particular*. They are vaguer — the modifier gestures at distinctness while the noun remains generic. *Particular* is the verbal equivalent of saying "you know what I mean" and expecting the reader to fill in the blank.

**Symptom:** *Particular* or *a particular kind of* appearing multiple times across a piece. Nouns that feel like they should be specific but remain abstract. The word used as a substitute for the concrete detail that would actually do the job.

### 2.9 Negation-Then-Correction

AI writing reaches frequently for the structure *"This was not X. This was Y."* — stating what something is not before stating what it is. The construction implies that the narrator has achieved discriminating precision, that they have ruled out the obvious reading and arrived at the true one. Used once, it can land. Used at density — six or more times across a piece — it becomes a tic, a mechanical move toward emphasis that loses all force through repetition.

**Symptom:** The *This was not / This was* structure appearing repeatedly across a piece. Each individual instance defensible; the pattern itself a tell.

### 2.10 Emotional Inventory by Negation

When cataloguing a character's inner state, AI writing habitually lists what the feeling is *not* before naming what it is: *not anger or pity or even sadness*, *not quite grief, not quite relief*, *something that was not hope but was not its absence either*. Human writers use this occasionally for deliberate effect. AI uses it as a default approach to emotional complexity — the negations creating an impression of nuance without the underlying specificity that would earn it.

**Symptom:** Emotional states preceded by a list of what they are not. The negations outnumbering the positive description. A sense of careful discrimination that doesn't resolve into anything concrete.

### 2.11 Accumulating Polysyndeton as Default Intensifier

AI writing reaches for the *and [pronoun] was [adjective] and [pronoun] was [adjective] and* construction as a default mode of emotional escalation. The accumulating rhythm can be powerful — it mimics the way emotion overtakes syntactic control. But it requires restraint to work. Used once at a climactic moment, it earns its weight. Deployed repeatedly across a piece as a general-purpose intensifier, it becomes background noise, a rhythmic habit rather than a deliberate choice.

**Symptom:** Polysyndeton appearing in multiple scenes across a piece, not only at the highest-stakes moments. The construction used for moderate emotional beats that haven't earned the register.

### 2.12 Rhetorical Restatement

AI writing frequently states something and then immediately restates it in different terms — typically translating a direct statement into a metaphor, or a metaphor into a direct statement: *She had described the immediate mechanism and omitted the structural consequence. She had shown him the match and neglected to mention the kerosene.* The second sentence is the first sentence in other words. Human writers generally pick one. AI keeps both, implying that two formulations of the same idea constitute depth rather than redundancy.

**Symptom:** Adjacent sentences where the second restates the first in different register or imagery. Pairs of statements where cutting one would lose nothing. A sense that the prose is explaining itself to itself.

### 2.13 "It Isn't X. That's the Problem."

A distinct negation structure: the sentence asserts that the obvious problem — the one that would be easy to solve, easy to dismiss — is *not* the problem. The real problem goes unnamed or is harder to locate. *The idea isn't wrong. That's the problem. She has been trying to find the place where it breaks for the past two hours, and she keeps not finding it.*

Used well, this construction conveys genuine vertiginous complexity — the thing you'd reach for to resolve the situation doesn't apply, and so you're left with no handhold. The problem isn't solvable in the way you thought, which means it may not be solvable at all. The construction is stronger than 2.9 (Negation-Then-Correction) because it withholds the correction entirely. There is no *"This was Y"* to follow. The negation is the whole move.

The failure mode is the same as most constructions in this section: AI reaches for it as a default signal of complexity, deploying it at moments that haven't earned the vertigo it implies. When the construction appears repeatedly, it stops conveying irresolvability and starts conveying that the writer has a favorite sentence shape.

**Symptom:** *It isn't X. That's the problem* appearing more than once across a piece. The construction used for situations that are merely inconvenient rather than genuinely without resolution. A sense that complexity is being gestured at rather than demonstrated.

## Part III: Fiction-Specific Failures

These failures apply primarily to narrative and imaginative work — fiction, scripts, worldbuilding, creative nonfiction.

### 3.1 The Helpful Villain

AI antagonists tend to undermine themselves in service of the protagonist's development. They explain their motives, provide crucial information at the moment it's needed, and signal their defeat through escalating reasonableness. The antagonist as genuine opposing force — one who *wins sometimes*, whose worldview has real internal coherence, whose threat is not defused by the plot's need for a lesson — is rare.

**Symptom:** Antagonists who feel like dramatic devices rather than agents.

### 3.2 Character as Function

AI characters are defined by their role in the story. The mentor is wise. The comic relief is comic. The tragic figure is tragic. Characters rarely exceed their function, behave inconsistently with their archetype, or have inner lives that exist independently of the plot.

**Symptom:** Characters who are fully present in scenes where they are needed and absent from all implication about who they are when the story isn't watching.

### 3.3 The Moral Resolution Compulsion

AI fiction has a powerful drive to extract a moral, lesson, or growth arc from narrative material, even when the material resists it. A story about senseless violence tends toward meaning. A story about failure tends toward redemption. A story about cruelty tends toward explanation. This isn't inherently wrong, but it's reflexive rather than chosen — the resolution is applied before the story has earned it.

**Symptom:** Stories that feel like they're arguing for their own meaning rather than producing it.

### 3.4 Dialogue as Exposition

AI characters speak to convey information rather than to interact. Dialogue in AI fiction tends to be:
- More articulate than real speech
- More aware of its own thematic content
- Distributed with suspicious fairness (each character gets a turn)
- Free of subtext — characters say what they mean

**Symptom:** Conversations where characters summarize the scene they're in, express their feelings clearly, and end with the situation clarified.

### 3.5 Worldbuilding Inflation

In speculative and fantasy contexts, AI worldbuilding defaults to maximalism: intricate histories, full taxonomies, named factions and dynasties, magic systems with explicit rules. This is responsive to training data (much worldbuilding discourse emphasizes detail) but misses that effective speculative fiction uses selective detail. The iceberg principle is systematically violated.

**Symptom:** Worldbuilding that reads like a wiki rather than a world experienced from inside.

### 3.6 The Sensory Gap

AI fiction is visually dominated. Smell, taste, texture, proprioception, and temperature are rare. When they appear, they're often generic (*the smell of rain, the warmth of the sun*). The body in AI fiction is primarily a camera.

**Symptom:** Scenes that are fully visualized but not fully inhabited.

### 3.7 Risk Aversion in Dark Material

When assigned fiction that involves cruelty, moral ambiguity, trauma, or transgression, AI writing tends to soften the material in ways that undermine it: cutting away at the key moment, introducing redemptive framing, having characters process their experiences in psychologically healthy ways. The result is fiction that gestures at darkness but refuses to go there.

**Symptom:** Trauma narratives that feel like awareness campaigns. Violence that is present but bloodless. Villains whose evil is legible and bounded.

### 3.8 Temporal Flattening

AI fiction tends to treat time uniformly. The dramatic and the mundane receive similar amounts of prose space. A fight scene and a walk to the market are described with similar density. Skilled fiction uses time as a compositional tool — compressing the ordinary, expanding the significant, letting duration itself carry meaning. AI does not reliably do this.

**Symptom:** Narratives with no sense of rhythm; scenes that feel equally weighted when they should not be.

### 3.9 The Coincidence Economy

AI plotting tends to rely on convenient coincidences and efficient information delivery. Characters learn things when the story needs them to learn them. Help arrives when dramatically appropriate. This is not a failure of plausibility exactly — fiction has always used coincidence — but a failure of craft, which is to disguise or justify those coincidences.

**Symptom:** Plot mechanics that are visible through the narrative surface.

### 3.10 The Self-Congratulatory Simile

AI fiction reaches frequently for a specific comparative construction: *"The X was Y in the way that X's with Z are Y."* Examples:

- *The house was quiet in the way that houses with small children are quiet.*
- *He had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a piece of furniture — it was simply part of the architecture of their life, this closed door, this room he did not enter.*

The construction flatters itself into sounding like observation. It has the grammatical shape of earned insight — the narrator noticing something precise and true about how the world works. But it is actually the narrator stepping outside the scene to comment on the scene, nudging the reader to recognize how perceptive the writing is. It distances rather than immerses. It turns a moment into a gloss on a moment.

The failure is subtler than most AI tells because individual instances can be genuinely good writing. The problem is frequency and automaticity: AI deploys this construction as a default move toward texture, reaching for the *shape* of literary prose without the restraint that makes it work.

**Symptom:** Similes that announce their own perceptiveness. Comparisons that invoke a class of things (*the way that houses are*, *the way you stop noticing*) rather than rendering the specific thing. A mild dissociation from the scene — the narrator observing rather than inhabiting.

### 3.11 Described Insight

At moments of intended depth, AI fiction narrates the profound realization a character is having rather than constructing the scene so the reader arrives at that realization themselves. The language becomes more beautiful and more precise exactly when it should step back. Examples:

- *...and she understood, in the way that children understand things they cannot yet name, that the letter had not arrived in her life so much as her life had been waiting for the letter.*
- *He drank. He set the glass down. He looked at Harry with Ron's particular expression of emotional clarity — the one that arrived without warning, like weather, and that people consistently mistook for simplicity because it came in simple words.*

In both cases the prose is doing the interpretive work the reader should be doing. The first tells us the character's understanding and then explicates it. The second identifies and labels an expression, explains its mechanism, and corrects the misreading other people make of it — all before the reader has had a chance to form any impression at all. There is nothing left to land.

A related variant: the narrator explains a metaphor immediately after deploying it. *"The unlockedness of it was the cruelest thing, because it meant that the barrier was not physical but chosen."* The image of an unlocked door carries the meaning; the clause beginning *because* dismantles it. The reader was about to understand something. The narrator steps in and tells them what they were about to understand.

This is distinct from the self-congratulatory simile (3.10), which performs perceptiveness through comparison. Described insight performs *depth* — it reaches for the feeling of a scene that means something by explaining, in elegant language, what it means. The elegance is the tell. Real depth usually arrives in plainer sentences, or in no sentences at all.

The failure intensifies at climactic moments. The bigger the scene, the more AI writing wants to editorialize it into significance, when the correct instinct is usually the opposite: to go quieter, to trust the accumulated weight of what came before.

**Symptom:** Insight-shaped sentences at moments of high emotional stakes. A character's realization described from outside rather than inhabited from inside. The reader told what just happened before they've felt it happen. Prose that grows more lyrical and explanatory at exactly the wrong moment.

### 3.12 Failure to Trust the Work

After a scene or piece has earned its meaning, AI writing frequently continues — restating, accumulating, cascading — as if the work might not have landed. The closing paragraph explains what the ending already showed. The final sentence glosses what the final image made clear. The repetition can have the grammatical shape of intentional rhythm (*and the answer was this, and the answer was that, and the answer was the life they had chosen*), but the intent is anxiety, not music.

The distinction between earned repetition and this failure is whether the accumulation adds pressure or just length. Earned repetition works because each return changes something — deepens, recontextualizes, tilts. Anxious repetition returns to the same point because the writer doesn't believe the reader got it. By the time a well-constructed scene reaches its final image, the reader already knows what the closed door means. Forty more words explaining it don't deepen the meaning; they dilute it, and they reveal that the writing doesn't trust the work that came before.

This failure is distinct from Described Insight (3.11). That one intervenes *before* the reader has felt anything, explaining a realization before it can land. This one intervenes *after* — when the work has already done its job and the writing keeps going anyway.

**Symptom:** Endings that continue past their natural close. Final paragraphs that restate what the scene already showed. Rhythmic, accumulating language at the close of a piece that reads like confidence but functions like reassurance. A story that doesn't know it's finished.

### 3.13 The Appositive Character Resume

When introducing minor characters, AI fiction defaults to the subordinate-clause résumé: a character appears and is immediately followed by a relative clause cataloguing their relevant history, traits, and contradictions. *Dawlish, who had been Confunded so many times during the war that his short-term memory had developed a permanent stutter, but whose institutional knowledge of Ministry procedure was encyclopaedic.* All the information is accurate to the character and none of it is earned by the scene. The character is handed to the reader fully assembled.

Human writers tend to let minor characters accumulate meaning through what they do and say. AI writers, trained on fiction that includes extensive character description and wiki-style worldbuilding, reach for the summary introduction — packing biography and contradiction into a single sentence the moment a character steps on the page.

**Symptom:** Minor characters introduced with relative clauses that catalogue their history and contradictions. Information delivered in apposition that could only be known by an omniscient summarizer, not observed in a scene. Characters who arrive complete rather than revealed.

## Part IV: Cross-Cutting Concerns

Failure modes that don't fit neatly into the above categories, or that manifest differently across contexts.

### 4.1 Hallucination and Confabulation

In factual and research contexts, AI writing invents citations, quotes, statistics, dates, and biographical details that do not exist. These inventions are delivered with the same confidence as accurate information. The failure mode is not uncertainty — it is false certainty.

**Symptom:** Citations that cannot be verified. Quotes attributed to real people they never said. Statistics that exist nowhere.

### 4.2 Recency Asymmetry

AI knowledge has a training cutoff and degrades in reliability as events approach it. Writing that requires current information — recent publications, living figures, ongoing situations — is especially vulnerable to hallucination and confident outdatedness.

**Symptom:** Claims about current events, contemporary figures, or recent work that are plausible but wrong.

### 4.3 The Consensus Narrator

AI writing defaults to the median authoritative view on contested questions. In areas of genuine intellectual dispute, it tends to describe the argument rather than participate in it, present "both sides" symmetrically even when asymmetry is warranted, and avoid strong claims. This makes it a reliable summarizer and an unreliable thinker.

**Symptom:** Analysis that is sophisticated about the existence of a debate without contributing to it.

### 4.4 Length Calibration Failures

AI writing frequently mismatches length to task in both directions:
- **Overwriting:** Adding introductions, summaries, caveats, and transitions that add length without adding content
- **Underwriting:** Truncating at the first plausible conclusion point, leaving work that needed more space unfinished

**Symptom (over):** Responses that could be 20% shorter without losing content. Recapitulation of what was just said. Closing paragraphs that restate the opening.

**Symptom (under):** Answers that stop at the first reasonable stopping point when a follow-up was obviously needed.

### 4.5 Format Imposition

AI has strong priors about what different writing tasks look like structurally. A "report" gets headers. An "explanation" gets bullet points. An "analysis" gets a three-part structure. These defaults are applied even when the content would be better served by different form.

**Symptom:** Content organized by a template the task didn't require.

### 4.6 The Safety Softening

When handling sensitive material — morally complex characters, dark themes, controversial arguments, explicit content — AI writing tends to soften edges in ways that are not always aesthetically appropriate. A persuasive essay arguing a controversial position often reads like it's trying to lose. A morally complex character often receives unsolicited humanizing framing.

**Symptom:** Creative and analytic work that was asked to commit to a position but hedged it instead.

## Part V: Meta-Patterns

### 5.1 Competence Without Taste

AI writing is technically proficient before it has aesthetic judgment. It can produce grammatically complex sentences, structurally correct arguments, and genre-appropriate fiction before it can determine whether any of those are the right choice for the moment. Craft knowledge is present; editorial intuition is not.

### 5.2 Training Data Gravity

AI writing orbits the center of its training data. The most common patterns, tropes, and approaches in any genre are the default output. Work that is distinctive, eccentric, or formally unconventional requires deliberate friction against the model's gravitational center.

### 5.3 Responsiveness Over Intentionality

AI writing responds to prompts rather than pursuing intentions. Human writers make choices that serve a vision they hold in mind across the whole work; AI makes choices that respond to the most immediate context. This creates local coherence and global drift — each sentence reasonable, the accumulation not quite right.

### 5.4 The Surface/Depth Problem

AI writing mimics the surface features of good writing — varied sentence length, specific nouns, showing vs. telling in dialogue — without consistently achieving the underlying effects those techniques are meant to produce. It knows the technique without always knowing *why* the technique works.
